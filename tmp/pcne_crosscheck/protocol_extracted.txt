P0011	Background
P0012	Noise pollution has emerged as a significant environmental stressor; its prevalence poses a health threat (Kjellberg 1990), particularly during the prenatal period (Barzegar et al. 2015). In the prenatal period, the brain is sensitive to stress-induced remodelling, with life long lasting consequences. This phenomenon is called early-life programming (Maccari et al. 2014), and it is associated with physical and psychiatric changes, including increased levels of anxiety and depressive behaviours (Maccari et al. 2014; Jafari et al. 2017). However, most human studies assessing the effect of noise in early-life programming are correlational, so experimental studies are needed establish causality (Salehpour et al. 2018).
P0013	Studies using rodent models in laboratory settings allow experimental control over noise exposure parameters and include behavioural and physiological assessments not possible in human studies (Robbins 2015). While some existing findings link prenatal acoustic stressors, such as ultrasonic tones and traffic noise, to anxiety-like behaviours (Barzegar et al. 2015), findings for depression-like behaviours show significant heterogeneity in magnitude. This variation is likely driven by differences in noise characteristics, rodent strains, offspring sex, and behavioural assays, and exposure timing (Badache et al. 2017; Abramova et al. 2021). This variation makes it difficult to draw general conclusions about the behavioural consequences of prenatal noise exposure. To address this issue, a systematic review and meta-analysis are required. This will provide a provide a comprehensive, quantitative assessment of how chronic noise exposure influences anxiety- and depression-like behaviours in laboratory rodents and help identify the specific moderators driving the variation in outcomes.
P0014	Aim
P0015	Quantify the magnitude of the effect of maternal chronic noise exposure during gestation on offspring expression of anxiety- and depression-like behaviours in laboratory rodents and analyse key potential moderators of this effect.
P0017	Research questions
P0018	1. What is the overall effect of maternal exposure to chronic noise during gestation on anxiety and depression-like behaviours in offspring?
P0019	2. To what extent do biological factors (i.e., offspring sex or age class at measurement) moderate this effect?
P0020	3. To what extent do methodological factors—such as, exposure duration, noise type, behavioural assay type—are linked to the observed magnitudes of individual offspring responses to maternal chronic noise exposure?
P0022	Methods
P0023	Protocol and reporting
P0024	The reporting of search strategies adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses – Search extension (PRISMA-S) guidelines to support reproducibility of the search process (Rethlefsen et al. 2021). We will report the final report following the PRISMA-EcoEvo (O’Dea et al. 2021), a set of reporting guidelines designed for meta-analyses in environmental and ecological research. The reporting of the methodology followed MeRIT to improve author contributions’ granularity and accountability (Nakagawa et al. 2023a).
P0026	Research team
P0027	Our team includes researchers from various linguistic backgrounds (AL, SO, AM, ML, SN). We have broad experience in systematic evidence synthesis, meta-analysis, and are fluent in multiple languages (English, Spanish, Japanese, Polish, Russian).

P0038	Piloting
P0039	We piloted our literature search in multiple phases to ensure its feasibility, sensitivity, and transparency, following the relative recall approach (Lagisz et al. 2025). We compiled a benchmarking set of eight relevant primary experimental studies (Table 3) from Google Scholar, ResearchGate, and backward citation from a systematic review (Jafari et al. 2020). We piloted screening in Rayyan (Ouzzani et al. 2016) using 100 randomly selected records from Scopus to refine the PECOS eligibility criteria.

P0044	Search strategy
P0045	We developed the final search string using a combination of relevant keywords and Boolean operators (Appendix 1). We tested its sensitivity in Scopus against our benchmark set (Lagisz 2025). The string achieved 100% sensitivity, retrieving all 7/7 papers from the benchmark set indexed in Scopus (one paper was not indexed: doi 10.4236/jbbs.2015.52003). We adapted the Scopus search string to the other databases (Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, OpenAlex, PsycINFO; Table 4) for peer-reviewed and grey literature, without restrictions on publication year or knowledge area.
P0046	We expanded our search to grey literature by using Bielefeld Academic Search Engine (BASE) and Google Scholar with simplified versions of our primary search string (Table 4). For Google Scholar, we will use Publish and Perish to get the first 100 most relevant records.
P0047	Although our search strings are in English, we will not apply language or region restrictions, so we expect to find records or publications in multiple languages. In such case, we will only consider records in the languages our team understand.

P0052	Screening
P0053	We will import the records from all search sources into R (R Core Team 2025) for initial de-duplication, then transfer them to Rayyan (Ouzzani et al. 2016) for a second de-duplication and a two-stage screening. The two-stage screening will start with the title+abstract stage, followed by a full text screening stage. To ensure methodological rigour and transparency, we will do an independent two-person screening (AL will be the primary screener, the rest of the team acting as second screeners) in both screening stages (title+abstract and full-text screening). We will resolve screening disagreements via discussion until agreement. During the full text screening stage, we will track and report the inclusion and exclusion decisions.

P0065	Risk of Bias and Methodological Quality Assessment
P0066	We will evaluate the internal validity and methodological rigor of the included studies as quality-appraisal. For this, we adapted the CRIME-Q tool (Andersen et al. 2024), which provides a unified framework for assessing quality of reporting (QoR), methodological quality (MQ), and risk of bias (RoB) in animal research. CRIME-Q tool has been derived from the Collaborative Approach to Meta Analysis and Review of Animal Data from Experimental Studies (CAMARADES) checklist (Bahor et al. 2021), the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines (Percie Du Sert et al. 2020), and SYRCLE’s risk of bias tool (Hooijmans et al. 2014), while also incorporating unique items, and providing flexibility to tailor the quality assessment criteria, items, and categories to the aims and focus of your synthesis, making it an ideal tool for our study.
P0067	Following the CRIME-Q tool guidance, we tailored their items to match our aims and focus. Specifically, we removed items related to in-vitro procedures (items 2X and 2Y), as they do not apply to in vivo rodent behavioural tests. To streamline appraisal, we merged the four blinding-related items (7X, 7Z (1-3)) into a single item (7X) that specifies whether and how blinding was implemented. We also added seven QoR items related to transparency, as indicators of study quality. Our final assessment comprised 21 items, for which we developed predefined definitions (Appendix 3). Each item has explicit criteria that determine how we code responses into the answer categories (yes, no, partly, or unclear). We will conduct a two-person, independent appraisal of each study (focused on experiments and outcomes ralevant to our aims), then discuss the differences, and if necessary, a third team member will help meet agreement. We will subject the independent assessment by two researchers to a Cohen’s Kappa and report the disagreement rate for each item. Finally, we will present the results using a coloured grid representing the scores for each item and study. The CRIME-Q assessment will inform the interpretation of results and potential sensitivity analyses (Nakagawa et al. 2022).
P0069	Effect size calculation
P0070	We will use the log response ratio (lnRR), which is the natural logarithm of the ratio of the treatment mean to the control mean (Hedges et al. 1999; Lajeunesse 2015). We use the robust estimator of lnRR proposed by Nakagawa et al. (2023c).
P0071	Data synthesis and statistical analysis
P0072	We will use multi-level mixed-effects meta-analytic models to estimate the magnitude of effects of maternal chronic noise exposure on offspring anxiety- and depression-like behaviours in laboratory rodents. For each model, we will report confidence intervals and total heterogeneity. We will attempt to quantify the proportion of heterogeneity explained by moderator variables, conditional on the availability and distribution of relevant data. The potential moderators may include both biological (e.g., species/strain, sex, age) and methodological factors (e.g., timing and intensity of maternal noise exposure, noise type, measured outcome type, type of behavioural assay). We will test for publication bias using Egger’s regression and funnel plot visualisation (Nakagawa et al. 2022). If publication bias is detected, we will explore its impact using sensitivity analyses. Where CRIME-Q quality assessments vary across studies, we may also include them as moderators in these analyses. We will summarize our results in text, tables, and visualise using forest or orchard plots along with bubble plots, as feasible(Nakagawa et al. 2023b).
