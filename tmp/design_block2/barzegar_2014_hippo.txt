Prenatal Exposure to Noise Stress: Anxiety, Impaired Spatial Memory,
and Deteriorated Hippocampal Plasticity in Postnatal Life
Marzieh Barzegar, Fatemeh Sadat Sajjadi, Sayyed Alireza Talaei,
Gholamali Hamidi, and Mahmoud Salami*
ABSTRACT: Sound pollution is known as an annoying phenomenon in
modern life. Especially, development of organisms during fetal life is
more sensitive to environmental tensions. To address a link between the
behavioral and electrophysiological aspects of brain function with
action of hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis in stressed animals,
this study was carried out on the male Wistar rats prenatally exposed to
sound stress. Groups of pregnant rats were exposed to noise stress for
1, 2, and 4 hour(s). The degree of anxiety and the spatial memory were
evaluated by elevated plus maze and Morris water maze, respectively.
Basic synaptic activity and long-term potentiation (LTP) induction were
assessed in the CA3-CA1 pathway of hippocampus. The serum level of
corticosterone was measured in the pregnant mothers and the offspring.
The behavioral experiments appeared that the stressed animals per-
formed considerably weaker than the control rats. The prenatal stress
negatively affected the basic synaptic responses and led to a lower level
of LTP. The pregnant animals showed an increased serum corticosterone
in comparison with the nonpregnant females. Also the offspring exposed
to the noise stress had a more elevated level of corticosterone than the
control rats. Our ﬁndings indicate that the corticosterone concentration
changes markedly coincides the results of behavioral and electrophysio-
logical experiments. We conclude that, similar to other environmental
stresses, the sound stress during fetal life efﬁciently disturbs both cogni-
tive abilities and synaptic activities. The changes in action of HPA axis
may contribute to problems of the brain function in the prenatally stress
exposed animals.

INTRODUCTION
Experimental researches conﬁrm that during prenatal period the
development of an organism is sub jected to complex environmental
inﬂuence (Lordi et al., 1997). Copio us animal studies have indicated
that maternal stress during pregnancy leave a range of long-term effect
on the offspring (Weinstock, 2001). Although sound
is invaluable in communication of most creatures
including human beings, however, undesirable,
unpleasant, or hurtful so unds are known as noise.
Noise is one of the most widespread sources of envi-

is reported that prenatal exposure to noises nega-
tively inﬂuenced the development of fetus as well as
neonate (Kjellberg, 1990). Increased ante partum
fetal death, congenital anomaly in the central nerv-
ous system, impaired social behavior in juvenile
stage, and the long-term alteration in the immune
function have been attributed to prenatal exposure
to noise (Sobrian et al., 1997; Kay et al., 1998).
Increased response in hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal
(HPA) axis is also reported in stressful situations
(Henry et al., 1994). It is demonstrated that stress-
induced secretion of glucocorticoid hormones by
adrenal cortex inﬂuence cognitive performance (Roo-
zendaal, 2002). Physiological and behavioral modiﬁ-
cations under stress are supposed to be depend on
developmental actions of glucocorticoids; lipid solu-
ble hormones that may pass from maternal into fetal
circulation (T akahashi et al., 1988). T wo receptor
systems for corticosterone are found in rat brain:
mineralocorticoid receptors and glucocorticoid recep-
tors known as Type I and Type II receptors, respec-
tively. As the prenatal brain development is highly
sensitive phase of developing brain, the excessive
amount of glucocorticoids at critical periods of fetal
life can modify neuroendocrine functions and the
morphology of several brain structures, including
hippocampus in the limbic system (Matthews,
2000).
Hippocampal formation that begins in the prena-
tal life and continues to the postnatal period
(Gould and T anapat, 1999) is crucial for animals to
use spatial information to guide and organize their
behavior (Silvers et al., 2003). Evidence indicates
that prenatal stresses effectively affect spatial mem-
ory (Yaka et al., 2007) as well as synaptic plasticity
(Yang et al., 2006) in offspring. The present study
was designed to evaluate the effect of prenatal noise
stress on the experimental form of synaptic plastic-
ity, spatial representations and anxiety in postnatal
period of life.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Animals Breeding and Ethics
Virgin female Wistar rats on the night of proestrus were
mated with male Wistar rats. The next day, the cage was
explored for a vaginal plug, and if found, this day was consid-
ered as the ﬁrst day of pregnancy. Pregnant rats were randomly
allocated to one of four (see below) experimental conditions
(one animal per cage). Free access to food and water and con-
trolled room conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, light on at 6:00
a.m., temperature 22
/C14 C, and air humidity 55–60%) were
maintained throughout the experiments. Efforts were made to
minimize the number of animals used and their suffering.
Adequate measures were taken to minimize animal’s pain or
discomfort. The experiments were approved by Kashan Univer-
sity of Medical Sciences’ Ethical Committee and were carried
out in accordance with the Directive 2010/63/EU on the pro-
tection of animals used for scientiﬁc purposes.
Noise Induction
Broad band trafﬁc noise was recorded in a high-trafﬁc square
in capital of Iran (T ehran) by a standard recorder (Panasonic
RQ-L11) and ampliﬁed by a loud speaker (Power 56W). As
level of 95 dB (A) is comparable with the noise level detected
in some industrial workplaces the intensity of the sound was
set at this intensity by a software (Sonar, Cakewalk). A preci-
sion sound level meter (Extech Instruments, MA) was used to
monitor the intensity of sound to 95 dB (A) uniformly in the
cage. Stress group animals were exposed to the noise (1, 2, and
4 h per day for 7 days) during 08:00–14:00 daily.
Noise Exposure
The rat home cages were placed in a reﬂective Plexiglas
chamber with these dimensions: 60 cm ( w) 3 60 cm ( d) 3
90 cm ( h). The loud speaker was installed upper left at a dis-
tance of 30 cm above the cage.
Pregnant and Nonpregnant Animals
All pregnant rats (except controls) at the third trimester of
gestation were exposed daily to the noise stress. Three groups
of pregnant animals were received the white noise for 1 (PN1),
2 (PN2), or 4 (PN4) h (s). During this period of time the
serum level of corticosterone is the lowest (Dallman et al.,
2000). Another pregnant group as control pregnant animals
was kept in the noise chamber for the same period of time
without noise exposure. The values from the latter group were
also compared with a group of nonpregnant female rats.
Offspring
After weaning only the male offspring rats were kept and
raised till adulthood. The offspring prenatally exposed to 1, 2,
and 4 h noise were assigned to N1, N2, and N4, respectively.
Pups born from the pregnant control rats were assigned as con-
trol (CON). The litters at 42–50 days of postnatal age were
entered the experiments. Generally, one to two pups were taken
from each mother. The N1, N2, N4, and CON rats (one
group of each, n 5 10 in each group) were introduced to the
behavioral (elevated plus maze and Morris water maze) testing.
Groups of N1, N2, N4, and CON animals were also entered
the electrophysiological experiments.
Behavioral Experiments
Spatial memory performance
The spatial memory tests were performed using a Morris
water maze (a tank made of galvanized metal, 150 cm diame-
ter, 70 cm depth) ﬁlled with water (22 /C14 C) up to 20 cm below
the rim with visual cues on the surrounding room wall. The
pool was divided into four equal quadrants named northeast,
southeast, northwest and southwest. A circular platform
(10 cm diameter) was submerged 1.5 cm below the water sur-
face and located in the center of one quadrant in constant
position throughout the experiment. In the training sessions
the rats were given four spatial trials/day for four consecutive
days. On each trial, the rat had 90 s to ﬁnd the hidden plat-
form. If it failed, the animal was guided to the platform and
allowed to rest on it for 20 s before being gently dried and
moved to a holding cage for the about 10 min intertrial inter-
val. The releasing locations were changed from trial to trial.
The probe trials were conducted on day 5, 24 h after the ﬁnal
training session in which the platform was removed, and the
rats were released from a ﬁxed starting position and allowed to
swim freely for 90 s before being removed. Performance on the
training and probe trial phases was measured by escape latency
(the time from release of animal in the maze until it located
the hidden platform) and percent time (the time spent by the
rat in target quadrant where the platform was formerly placed),
respectively. All experiments were carried out at night time.
During testing, the rat was videotaped from a ﬁxed overhead
camera, and custom software (Radiab 7) was used to score
performance.
Anxiety evaluation
The elevated plus maze evaluates spatial anxiety in rats (Car-
obrez and Bertoglio, 2005; Haller and Alicki, 2005). Brieﬂy,
the apparatus was elevated 60 cm from the ﬂoor and contained
four 50 cm 3 10 cm arms and a 10 cm 3 10 cm center plat-
form. T wo opposite arms were enclosed by 40 cm walls, and
the other two arms were open. The testing room was moder-
ately illuminated. Each rat was placed in the central platform,
facing an open arm and was left to explore the maze for 5
min, while the behavior of animal being observed by the exper-
imenter. As a convention, an entry was deﬁned as having all
four paws in the arm. The parameters measured for the plus
maze navigation were the number of entries into open arms
(OAE) and the time spent in open arms (OAT). The percent
of OAE and OAT were calculated as follows:

In vivo electrophysiology
The animals were anesthetized with urethane (1.5 g/kg, i.p.)
and placed in a head holder in a stereotaxic frame. T wo small
holes (1 mm diameter) were drilled with a sterile drill bit in the
skull to allow the insertion of electrodes in the brain. Electrodes
were made from T eﬂon-coated stainless steel wire (0.008 inch
outside diameter) exposed only at the tip (tip separation
/C24 0.10 mm). A bipolar stimulating electrode (twisted T eﬂon-
insulated stainless steel) was lowered to the Schaffer collaterals (at
the coordinates anteroposterior (AP), 4.2; mediolateral (ML),
3.8 mm from Bregma; dorsoventral (DV), 2.8 mm from the
dural surface). A monopolar recording electrode was advanced
through the CA1 stratum radiatum (at the coordinates anteropos-
terior (AP), 3.4; mediolateral (ML), 2.5 mm from Bregma; dor-
soventral (DV), 2.5 mm from the dural surface). All coordinates
were based on Paxinos and Watson (Paxinos and Watson, 2007)
and adapted to the animals according to bregma-lambda distance
as previously described (T alaei and Salami, 2013). Extracellular
recordings were referenced to an indifferent site on the skull.
Constant current stimulation was programmed by a computer
software (Neurotrace, WSI, I.R. Iran) and delivered to a stimula-
tor/isolator unit (WPI-A365). Single-pathway recordings of the
evoked ﬁeld extracellular postsynaptic potentials (fEPSPs) were
made from the stratum radiatum in the CA1 area of the
hippocampus in response to stimulation (two sweeps/min at 30 s
intervals) of the ipsilateral Schaffer collateral-commissural path-
way. Once the response was stable, an input-output series was
recorded using a range of stimulus currents. Then, stimulation
intensity was adjusted to elicit a fEPSP slope of 60% of maxi-
mum. The baseline fEPSPs were recorded for a 30-min period
and averaged for comparison to post-tetanus recordings. Then,
long-term potentiation (L TP) was induced by high-frequency
stimulation (HFS) of 100 Hz (10 bursts of 10 stimuli, 0.2 ms
stimulus duration, and 10 s interburst intervals). Following the
tetanic stimulation, recordings continued for at least 2 h. The
fEPSPs were ampliﬁed on a preampliﬁer (Electromadule, WSI, I.R.
Iran), ﬁltered at 1–3,000 Hz, dig itized (10 points/ms) and stored
for ofﬂine analysis using a software (Potentalize, WSI, I.R. Iran)
running on a computer. The data w e r ec o n s i d e r e df o rt h ec h a n g e s
in slope of the pre- and post-tetani c responses. Data are presented
as mean 6 SEM and normalized by taking the pre-tetanus slope of
fEPSPs as 100% and comparing the post-tetanus fEPSPs with it.
Induction of LTP was deﬁned if mean slope of the fEPSP increased
by more than 20% after the HFS train. Blood samples were pooled
from the animals when the experiment ended.
Measurement of serum corticosterone
concentration
The blood samples for measuring the serum corticosterone
concentration were collected from the animal groups entered the
experiments and mothers either exposed or unexposed to the
sound stress. The blood samples were taken from: all pregnant
groups, the nonpregnant female group, the offspring entered
behavioral experiments before and after the testing, and the off-
spring used in electrophysiological experiments after recordings
ﬁnished. Animals were deeply anesthetized and blood was col-
lected from heart. The pooled blood was then collected into a
sterile tube (Kisker, Germany). The blood samples were centri-
fuged at 2,500 rpm for 10 min. The serum was collected and
frozen at 220
/C14 C until assayed. The serum corticosterone con-
centration was measured using a commercially available radioim-
munoassay (RIA) kit (DRG, Germany) according to the
manufacturer’s instruction. The manufacturer supplied protocol
was implemented. In brief, corticosterone level in serum samples
was single-determined via interpolation from a standard curve
derived from six calibrators (ranging from 25 to 1,500 ng/ml).
Data Analysis
One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with corticosterone
as between-subjects factor and noise exposure as within-subjects
factor was performed on the corticosterone level of serum. Also
one-way ANOVA with learning the water maze task, remem-
bering the learned task and the anxiety evaluation as between-
subjects factors and noise exposure as within-subjects factor
was carried out on the behavioral procedures. T wo-way
ANOVA with change in the slope of fEPSPs as between-
subjects factor and noise exposure as within-subjects factor was
applied to the electrophysiological experiments; however, the
potentiation was interpreted as LTP if the slope of fEPSP was
increased at least 20% post-tetanus. LSD was applied as post
hoc test. Data are expressed as mean 6 SEM. The basis of sta-
tistical decision was a signiﬁcance Alpha level of P < 0.05.

DISCUSSION
Copious reports have indicated an altered behavioral per-
formance in offspring born from mothers under stress during a
critical period of gestation. The cognitive changes are proved
to be associated with electrophysiological modiﬁcations in
some parts of brain. This study evaluates the effect of sound
stress in fetal life on spatial memory, HPA axis function, and
characteristics of synaptic transmission in the postnatal period
of life.
Behavioral Considerations
Our results demonstrated that the prenatal noise exposure
effectively weaken the behavioral performances during the post-
natal life. The negative effect of the sound stress was more pro-
nounced in the N2 and N4 groups. The animals in the N2
and N4 groups showed to perform reasonably lower than the
CON group in either plus maze navigation or Morris water
maze tasks. Anxiety of the animals was reﬂected in decreased
entering the open arms of plus maze and passing a shorter
time in these arms. Also we found that the stressed animals
swim a longer path for locating the hidden platform in the
water maze and spend a shorter navigation time in the target
quadrant; indicating an impaired spatial learning and memory.
Animal studies have demonstrated that maternal stress during
pregnancy give rise to a variety of long-term effect in offspring
including anxiety, learning impairments and reduced attention
(Igosheva et al., 2007). Some kinds of stress are reported to
efﬁciently impair spatial learning and memory abilities of the
offspring (Yaka et al., 2007). Acute stress disrupts both spatial
and recognition memory as assessed by a number of behavioral
tasks including radial arm maze, water maze, radial arm water
maze, and spontaneous recognition memory tests (Cazakoff
et al., 2010). Nishio et al. demonstrated that prenatal stress
exposure obviously inﬂuences the learning abilities in the off-
spring (Nishio et al., 2001). Prenatally stressed rats spend a
shorter time in the open arms of plus maze; demonstrating a
higher degree of anxiety (Murmu et al., 2006). Emotional and
cognitive deﬁcits are reported in the offspring born from moth-
ers exposed to stress during gestation (T alge et al., 2007).
Beside the studies conﬁrming our results some ﬁndings disagree
with the present work are also reported. Several researchers
found that prenatal stress either did not inﬂuence or decreased
learning performance in the adult offspring (Lordi et al.,
1997). This discrepancy in results could be due to some factors
including the task used, the stressor, the time and duration of
stress application and some characteristics of the animals used
in different studies such as sex and age (Joels, 2006).
Electrophysiological Considerations
Our results indicated that the sound pollution profoundly
affects the basic synaptic transmission. The noise stress
decreased the slope of pre-tetanus fEPSPs. Also, in contrast to
the control animals, the post-HFS responses recorded in all
groups prenatally exposed to the noise stress could not fulﬁll
the criterion of LTP induction in this study. Several reports
have demonstrated an impaired LTP and facilitated L TD in
hippocampal CA1 area in the prenatal stress exposed offspring
(Son et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2006). Yaka et al. reported a
diminished LTP as well as impaired spatial learning in the ani-
mals introduced to a series of prenatal stresses (Yaka et al.,
2007). Contrary to the studies just mentioned Fujioka et al.
reported that short-lasting and mild prenatal stress (30 min
daily restraining) promotes learning and, improves L TP and
neurogenesis in hippocampus of adult offspring. On the other
hand, they found that long-lasting and severe prenatal stress
lead to reverse results (Fujioka et al., 2006).
Hypothalamus-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis Function
Measurement of serum concentration of corticosterone
appeared that pregnancy itself highly increases secretion of the
hormone. Also the mothers under the noise stress and the off-
spring at the N2 and N4 groups had a higher serum level of
the glucocorticoid. These ﬁndings indicate that the noise pollu-
tion efﬁciently increases activity of the HPA axis in both moth-
ers and pups. On the other hand, the maze navigations
elevated the serum corticosterone in all groups of rats whether
exposed or unexposed to the stress. However, the hormone
surge was signiﬁcantly higher in the stressed rats compared
with the CON animals. The augmentation in the serum level
of the glucocorticoid was also observed in the animals entered
the electrophysiological experiments; however, the changes were
much less than in the behavioral testing and were statistically
signiﬁcant only in the N4 group. This could be rational as the
awaked animals under behavioral testing are much more stress-
ful than the anesthetized ones during the electrophysiological
recordings. T aken together, the behavioral performances and
the invasive in vivo experiments strengthen activity of the HPA
axis. From these considerations it can be concluded that the
anxiety and memory behavioral results from the maze perform-
ances coincides the synaptic transmission ﬁndings from the
electrophysiological recordings in that the higher the level of
corticosterone the lower the capability of cognitive function
and, basic and tetanized synaptic activity.
Concurrently , some human studies prove the glucocorticoid
hormone alterations during both pregnancy and fetal period. It
is demonstrated that maternal stress increases level of corticoster-
one either in mother or fetuses (T akahashi et al., 1998). How-
ever, cortisol levels rise in fetal serum remain about 13-fold
lower than in the mother (Gitau et al., 2001), because about
80% is metabolized by 11 b-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase

moderate levels of corticosterone enhance LTP . Moreover, they
found that lack of the hormone diminishes LTP occurrence
(Diamond et al., 1992). If corticosterone dose dependently
affect production of hippocampal LTP (Joels, 2006), needs
further investigation.
Probable Mechanisms Inﬂuencing Parts of Brain
Involved in Learning and Memory and LTP
It has been shown that stress exerts an intense impression on
molecular mechanisms underlying synaptic plasticity (Howland
and Wang, 2008). Both the hippocampal LTP and the hippo-
campal related spatial memory are known to be subject to plas-
ticity of synaptic transmission. The protocol of tetanization
and the recruited hippocampal circuit in the present study
expect a NMDA receptor dependent LTP . Positive, particularly
calcium, ions inﬂux through the NMDA receptors channel
founds cellular and molecular changes in the pre and postsy-
naptic neurons that leads to long lasting alterations in capabil-
ities of the synapse (Molnar, 2011). Changes in structure of
NMDA receptors including the combination of subunits might
affect their key function in the synaptic plasticity (Paoletti and
Neyton, 2007). Son et al. found that offspring of mice bearing
chronic immobilization stress during pregnancy efﬁciently
reduce the expression of NR1 and NR2B subunits of NMDA
receptor at the hippocampal synapses. Evidence indicates that
reduced expression of the NR2B subunits decreases ionic cur-
rents through NMDA receptor channel (Son et al., 2006). The
change in conductance of the receptor channel may affect the
NMDA receptor dependent LTP and, thus, hippocampus
dependent spatial memory (Son et al., 2006). Gerges et al
showed that stress diminishes expression of phosphorylated
calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II in hippocam-
pus (Gerges et al., 2004). These proteins are known to invol-
vein synaptic plasticity. There are evidences demonstrating that
prenatal stress perturbs biosynthesis of brain-derived neurotro-
phic factor (BDNF) in the hippocampus (Van den Hove et al.,
2006). BDNF is necessary for formation of the synapses and
synaptic plasticity as well (Cohen-Cory et al., 2010). From
these considerations it can be concluded that, through affecting
the expression or activation of some key molecular agents, the
prenatal stress might has a negative impact on the hippocampal
LTP and spatial memory.
The Stress-Induced Changes in Structure of
Hippocampus
The structural characteristics of the pathways involved in syn-
aptic plasticity and learning and memory phenomena are also
subjected to stress induced alterations. As hippocampus is rich
in glucocorticoid receptors (Zhang et al., 2013) the adrenal hor-
mones produced by stress could affect structure of hippocampus.
Accordingly, chronic exposure to either stress or stress-induced
glucocorticoids change structure of hippocampus in different
aspects including neurochemistry, excitability, neurogenesis, neu-
ronal morphology and cell death (for review see (Conrad,
2010)). Coe et al. reported a reduced volume of hippocampus
in nonhuman offspring prenatally exposed to unpredictable
noise (Coe et al., 2003). Concomitantly, Morales-Medina et al.
showed that 3 weeks administration of corticosterone in rats
causes dendritic remodeling and spine density reorganization in
pyramidal neurons of the CA1 area of the hippocampus
(Morales-Medina et al., 2009). Also in a histological study Bock
et al. showed that prenatal stress exposure give rise to shrinkage
of dendrites in either CA3 or CA1 areas of the hippocampus
(Bock et al., 2011). Therefore, stress or stress-induced hormone
corticosterone may affect L TP and, learning and memory
through some alterations in organization of the hippocampus.
The Inhibitory Role of Amygdala
Amygdala as a part of limbic system anatomically projects to
several hippocampal regions including the CA1 area (Yilmazer-
Hanke, 2008) and involve in stress behaviors and memory mod-
ulation. Stress is activating molecular plasticity in amygdala (Ilin
and Richter-Levin, 2009) and, amygdalo-hippocampal projec-
tions exert an inhibitory impact on synaptic plasticity in hippo-
campus (Akirav and Richter-Levin, 2006). Lesion to the
amygdala inhibits stress-induced deﬁcits of hippocampal L TP
and hippocampus mediated spatial memory in rats (Kim et al.,
2001). Moreover, application of GABA
A receptor agonist into
amygdala prevents stress-induced physiological and behavioral
results (Kim et al., 2005). In line, hippocampus of stressed ani-
mals receiving intra-amygdalar infusion of musimol displays a
substantial L TP comparable with control animals (Kim et al.,
2005). Thus, a declined L TP and cognitive function in the
stressed animals might be due to a stress activated inhibitory
impact of the amygdala on the hippocampus.
The Role of Oxidative Stressor
Oxidative stressors may contribute to stress-induced learning
and memory impairments (Sharma et al., 2009). Oxidative

CONCLUSIONS
T aken together, the prenatal noise stress endorses anxiety
and deeply undergoes the hippocampus linked learning and
memory processes at postnatal age. Also the sound pollution
during the fetal life pronouncedly inﬂuences the basic synaptic
activity as well as synaptic plasticity in the hippocampal circuits
of the offspring. Our ﬁndings are agreed to previous studies
assessing the effects of other stress protocols on cognitive func-
tions and synaptic speciﬁcities. Contribution of glucocorticoids
to various possible mechanisms involved in stress-induced
problems suggests that the prenatal sound stress may affect the
brain function more pronouncedly through reregulation of the
HPA axis action. Hence, considering a link between the sound
pollution and the HPA axis, future research could be focused
on the cognitive functions and its electrophysiological mecha-
nisms. However, impact of the other contributions, including
some neurotransmitter projections to the hippocampus, to del-
eterious effect of sound stress on fetal neurodevelopment need
to be considered.
